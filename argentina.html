Argentina in Summer: Iguazu Falls, Buenos Aires, El Calafate & Ushuaia — Christmas to New Year 2025

Two weeks, four destinations, one trip that completely reset our benchmark for what travel can be. Iguazu Falls alone was worth the flight.

Eleanor Roosevelt saw Iguazu Falls and said "Poor Niagara." We understood exactly what she meant the moment we rounded that first bend in the jungle path on the morning of December 24th and heard it before we saw it — a deep, bone-shaking roar that told us something extraordinary was about to happen. We spent Christmas at the most spectacular waterfall on Earth. New Year's Eve, we watched fireworks over the Beagle Channel from the End of the World. In between: Buenos Aires in its summer glory and a glacier the size of a small city. Two weeks. Four destinations. One of the best trips we've taken across 65 countries.

We travel mid-range: boutique hotels where possible, excellent food always, the occasional splurge when it's genuinely worth it. Argentina in peak summer (December–January) rewarded that approach at every stop — the nature was at its absolute best, the days were impossibly long, and the falls were thundering at full volume. This is the honest guide we wish we'd had.

Stop 01 of 04

Iguazu Falls

Nothing prepares you. That's not a cliché — it's a genuine fact about Iguazu. You can watch every video, study every photograph, read every account, and still emerge from the jungle path that first morning with your jaw unhinged and your ability to speak temporarily suspended. The falls are 275 individual cascades stretching nearly two miles across the Argentina-Brazil border. The centrepiece — La Garganta del Diablo, the Devil's Throat — is a U-shaped chasm that swallows 1,500 cubic metres of water every single second.

The noise hit us before the sight did. A deep, physical roar that you feel in your chest — like standing next to a speaker at a concert, but made entirely of water.

La Garganta del Diablo — the Devil's Throat. Arrive at 8am to see it before the crowds.

Which Side — Argentina or Brazil?

Visit both if you can, but if you only have one day, choose Argentina. The Argentine side has 80% of the falls and puts you directly in the middle of them — walkways thread through the jungle canopy and over the rushing water, placing you close enough to feel the spray permanently on your face. The Brazilian side offers the panoramic view: further away, but you can see the full sweep of the horseshoe in one glance. We spent two days on the Argentine side and half a day crossing to Brazil, and that was exactly right.

✦ Iguazu Falls — Everything You Need to Know

  • Arrive when the park opens at 8am. The Devil's Throat trail gets genuinely crowded by 10am.
  • Book tickets online in advance at the official park website — it's faster and you choose your entry time.
  • Entry fee (2025): around ARS 45,000 for non-residents — check current rates as Argentina's prices change frequently.
  • Do the Gran Aventura boat ride first thing so your clothes can dry while you walk the circuits. You will get completely soaked.
  • Bring a dry bag or waterproof case for your phone. The mist alone is enough to damage an unprotected camera.
  • The coatis (raccoon-like animals) will steal your food. Do not underestimate them. They are bold and organised.
  • We visited over Christmas and New Year (Dec 23–Jan 2, 2025) — peak summer in the Southern Hemisphere. The falls were absolutely thundering at full volume, the jungle was impossibly green and lush, and the wildlife was out in force. Highly recommend this window if you can get the time off.
  • The trade-off with peak summer: higher crowds mid-morning. Arrive at 8am sharp and you'll have the Devil's Throat almost to yourself for the first hour.
  • Full Moon nights: for five nights a month you can visit the falls under moonlight — magical and worth booking far in advance.
  • Stay 2 nights minimum in Puerto Iguazú to properly see both the Argentine and Brazilian sides.

Stop 02 of 04

Buenos Aires

Buenos Aires is one of those cities that gets under your skin. It's called the Paris of South America, and that's accurate in the sense that it has wide boulevards, excellent food and an air of cultural confidence — but it's also deeply, unmistakably itself. The neighbourhoods are the thing: San Telmo with its antique markets and cobblestones, Palermo Soho with its restaurants and tree-lined streets, La Boca with its painted houses and football-mad energy, Recoleta with its grand European cemetery where Evita is buried.

We gave Buenos Aires four days and it wasn't enough. We could have stayed a week.

🥩
📸 [Add your Buenos Aires photo here — La Boca, San Telmo or a parrilla]

Buenos Aires rewards slow walkers. The city reveals itself neighbourhood by neighbourhood.

What Not to Miss

The food, first. Eat steak every night if you can — at a proper parrilla (grill house), where the cuts come off wood-fire grills that have been running since noon. Don Julio in Palermo is consistently named the best parrilla in the city; book weeks in advance. La Brigada in San Telmo is the old-school choice — no-reservations, pure tradition. Order a bife de chorizo, a Malbec from Mendoza, and clear your schedule for the afternoon.

See a tango show if you're that way inclined, but the best tango in Buenos Aires isn't in a theatre — it's in the milongas, the community dance halls, where real dancers come to dance for each other. La Catedral in Almagro is the most atmospheric; anyone can come and watch from the edges.

✦ Buenos Aires — Practical Tips

  • Argentina has a complex currency situation — research the current official vs "blue" exchange rate before you go. Bringing USD cash can significantly stretch your budget.
  • The city is very safe by day in tourist neighbourhoods but use common sense at night, especially in La Boca after dark.
  • Dinner doesn't start until 9pm. Restaurants before 8pm will be empty. Embrace it.
  • Buy a SUBE card for the subway (subte) and buses — it's cheap and covers the whole city.
  • Allow at least half a day for the Recoleta Cemetery — it's one of the most extraordinary cemeteries in the world, not just because of Evita.
  • Fly domestically from Buenos Aires to your next destination. The city has two airports: Ezeiza (EZE) for international, Aeroparque (AEP) for domestic — most Patagonian flights use AEP.
🏨

Stop 03 of 04

El Calafate

El Calafate is a small, pretty town on the shore of the ice-blue Lago Argentino — and it exists, really, for one reason: the Perito Moreno Glacier. This is one of the only glaciers in the world that is not retreating. It advances, it calves enormous chunks of ice into the lake below with a crack like a rifle shot, and it has been doing this continuously for thousands of years. Standing on the viewing platforms in front of it — a wall of blue-white ice rising 60 metres above you — you understand immediately why people fly to the end of the Earth to see it.

The glacier calved while we were watching. A crack, a pause, and then a mass of ice the size of a building simply detached and fell into the lake, sending a wave across the surface and a roar up the valley walls.

🧊
📸 [Add your Perito Moreno Glacier photo here]

Perito Moreno Glacier, Los Glaciares National Park. Give yourself a full day — at least.

Minitrekking on the Glacier

You can walk on it. This is the thing that most people don't realise until they're there — guided minitrekking tours take you from the boat landing onto the glacier surface itself, crampons on, ice axes optional, walking across a landscape of extraordinary blue-white ridges and crevasses with guides who know every inch of it. It is one of the top five experiences of our entire 65-country journey. Book it through your hotel or directly with the park's concession operators well in advance, particularly in summer.

✦ El Calafate — What You Need to Know

  • Allow at least 2 full days in El Calafate — one for Perito Moreno, one for the glacier trek or a boat tour to Upsala and Spegazzini glaciers.
  • The glacier is 80km from town — join an organised tour or hire a car. There's no public bus directly to the park.
  • Book minitrekking well in advance. It sells out in summer (Nov–Feb) weeks ahead.
  • Fly into El Calafate (FTE) from Buenos Aires — 3-hour flight, daily services with Aerolíneas Argentinas, FlyBondi and JetSmart.
  • Try Calafate ice cream — the berry is local to Patagonia. It's genuinely extraordinary.
  • Day trips to El Chaltén (3 hours north) for world-class hiking around Mt Fitzroy are excellent if you have an extra day.
  • Summer brings near-24-hour daylight. The glacier at 9pm with golden light is extraordinary.

Stop 04 of 04

Ushuaia

The southernmost city in the world sits at the end of a channel, ringed by mountains, battered by a wind that comes straight off Antarctica. It is called El Fin del Mundo — the End of the World — and for once the nickname is entirely accurate. Standing on the shore of the Beagle Channel in Ushuaia, looking south across the water toward the Drake Passage and Antarctica, you feel the full, vertiginous realisation of how far you've come. Not just in miles, but in latitude, in imagination, in the sheer improbability of being somewhere so remote.

Ushuaia surprised us completely. We expected a bleak port town clinging to the edge of the map. We found a charming, surprisingly lively city with excellent restaurants, a beautiful national park on its doorstep, and some of the most dramatic scenery we've seen anywhere in sixty-five countries.

🌬️
📸 [Add your Ushuaia / Beagle Channel / Tierra del Fuego photo here]

The Beagle Channel from Ushuaia. On clear days you can see Navarino Island, Chile, on the far shore.

The Train at the End of the World

The Tren del Fin del Mundo is a narrow-gauge steam railway that runs through the Tierra del Fuego National Park — originally built by prisoners in the early 20th century to haul timber. Today it's a tourist attraction, but an atmospheric one: the little train puffs through beech forest and river valleys, and at the end of the line you can walk the trails of one of the world's most southerly national parks.

✦ Ushuaia — Everything You Need to Know

  • Direct flights from El Calafate with Aerolíneas Argentinas (1h 20min) save an exhausting overland journey. FlyBondi also flies 4 times weekly.
  • The Beagle Channel boat trip is the essential Ushuaia experience — you'll see sea lions, cormorants, penguins and the Les Eclaireurs lighthouse.
  • Tierra del Fuego National Park is 12km from the city centre. Take the park bus or hire a car.
  • Dress in layers. Always. Even in summer, the weather can swing from sunshine to sleet in an hour.
  • Buy a postcard from the post office here and get it stamped "Ushuaia — Fin del Mundo." It will be the most exciting postcard you've ever sent.
  • The "End of the World" sign is on the road heading south — get your photo there.
  • We visited in late December and early January — the height of Patagonian summer. Temperatures were a mild 10–14°C, the light lasted until nearly 11pm, and the landscapes were green and vivid rather than the stark winter grey. The long daylight hours meant we could hike until evening and still have golden light for photos.
  • The near-midnight golden light on the Beagle Channel at the end of December is something we will never forget. If you can visit over Christmas and New Year, do it.
  • From Ushuaia you can book Antarctica cruises — prices have dropped significantly. If it's on your bucket list, the Ushuaia ports are your gateway.

Suggested Argentina Itinerary

✦ Our Exact Itinerary — Dec 23, 2024 to Jan 2, 2025

  • Dec 23: Arrive Buenos Aires. Evening arrival at Ezeiza. Check into boutique hotel in Palermo. One quick empanada and collapse.
  • Dec 24–25: Buenos Aires — Christmas in the city. San Telmo antique market, Recoleta Cemetery, La Boca. Christmas dinner at a proper parrilla — steak and Malbec. Buenos Aires celebrates Christmas Eve in a big way; the streets were buzzing until 3am.
  • Dec 26–28: Iguazu Falls. Fly Buenos Aires → Puerto Iguazú (2hrs). The falls in full summer flood — the single best thing we've ever seen. Two full days Argentine side, half a day Brazil. The jungle was electric green and steaming.
  • Dec 29: Buenos Aires transit day. Fly back through Buenos Aires. Evening stroll through Palermo, final steak before Patagonia.
  • Dec 30: El Calafate. Fly Buenos Aires → El Calafate (3hrs). Perito Moreno glacier in afternoon light — jaw-dropping even after Iguazu.
  • Dec 31: New Year's Eve — Ushuaia. Fly El Calafate → Ushuaia (1h 20min). Beagle Channel boat trip in the afternoon. New Year's Eve fireworks over the channel at midnight — one of the most extraordinary New Year's Eves we've had across 65 countries.
  • Jan 1: Tierra del Fuego. New Year's Day in Tierra del Fuego National Park. The light at this latitude in summer lasts until 11pm. Golden hour lasted about three hours.
  • Jan 2: Fly home. Ushuaia → Buenos Aires → onwards. Already planning the return trip.

Christmas at Iguazu. New Year's Eve at the End of the World. We didn't plan it as a theme — but Argentina in summer turned out to be the most spectacular festive trip we've ever taken.

Costs & Practicalities

Argentina can be brilliant value or surprisingly expensive depending on how you handle the currency situation. The country has historically had multiple exchange rates — research the current situation before you travel, as it changes frequently. Bringing USD or EUR cash and exchanging at authorised exchange offices (not on the street) will typically get you a significantly better rate than using your bank card at ATMs.

✦ What We Spent Per Day (Mid-Range Budget)

  • Buenos Aires: $80–120/day (hotel, food, transport, one big dinner out)
  • Puerto Iguazú: $70–100/day (budget-friendly town, expensive park entry)
  • El Calafate: $120–180/day (add $60–100 for minitrekking)
  • Ushuaia: $100–150/day (boat tours, park entry, accommodation)
  • Domestic flights: $80–180 per leg depending on how far in advance you book

Was It Worth It?

Of all the countries we've visited — sixty-five now — Argentina in peak summer is one of the very few where the reality exceeded the expectation at every single stop. Going over Christmas and New Year turned out to be inspired timing we hadn't fully planned for: the nature was at its absolute peak, the days stretched on forever in the southern summer light, and celebrating Christmas Eve in Buenos Aires and New Year's Eve at the End of the World gave the whole trip a shape that still makes us smile when we think about it.

If you're wondering whether to go over the festive period — do it. Yes, it's peak season and you'll pay slightly more. But standing at Iguazu Falls on Christmas morning while the jungle steams around you, or watching New Year's fireworks from Ushuaia with the Beagle Channel glittering below — there are worse ways to see in a new year.

We've already talked about going back to do Mendoza and the wine country. Argentina gets under your skin that way.
